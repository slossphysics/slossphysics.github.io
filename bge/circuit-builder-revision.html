Circuit builder: revision

LI: I am revising how current and voltage behave in series and parallel circuits.

SC: I can measure current with an ammeter and voltage with a voltmeter · I can say how current and voltage share out in series and parallel circuits · I can explain what a resistor and an extra cell do to the current.

15:00
Tasks

1.Build a circuit with one cell and two lamps in series. Then drag one lamp off the grid. What happens to the other lamp?

Hint

Series means there is only one loop. The current goes through one lamp and then the other.

In series there is only one path, so taking one lamp out breaks the loop and the other lamp goes off too. Old strings of Christmas lights were wired like this: one bulb blows and they all go out.

2.Press Clear. Build a circuit with one cell and two lamps in parallel. Take one lamp out. What happens this time?

Hint

Parallel means each lamp has its own branch. Both ends of each lamp are joined to the cell.

Each lamp has its own branch, so taking one out leaves the other one lit, and just as bright. That is why the lights in a house are wired in parallel.

3.Press Clear. Build one cell and two lamps in series, with an ammeter on each side of the lamps. Type both readings.

Hint

An ammeter goes in series: drop it onto a wire and it joins into the loop.

Both read 0.15 A. In a series circuit the current is the same everywhere: no current is used up by the lamps.

4.Keep the two lamps in series. Put a voltmeter across lamp 1, then across lamp 2. Type both readings.

Hint

A voltmeter goes across a component: join one end of the voltmeter to one side of the lamp, and the other end to the other side.

Each lamp has 0.75 V. In a series circuit the voltage is shared between the components, and the shares add up to the cell’s voltage: 0.75 V + 0.75 V = 1.5 V.

5.Press Clear. Build two lamps in parallel on one cell. Put an ammeter in each lamp’s branch and one beside the cell. Write the three readings in your jotter. What do you notice?

Hint

Drop each ammeter onto a wire. Put one in each lamp’s branch, and one in the wire next to the cell.

Each branch has 0.30 A and the cell gives 0.60 A. In a parallel circuit the current splits between the branches: 0.30 A + 0.30 A = 0.60 A.

6.Keep the two lamps in parallel. Put a voltmeter across lamp 1, then across lamp 2. Type both readings.

Hint

Leave the circuit as it is. Join one end of the voltmeter to one side of a lamp, and the other end to its other side.

Each lamp has 1.50 V, the full voltage of the cell. In a parallel circuit the voltage is the same across every branch, so each lamp is as bright as one lamp on its own.

7.Press Clear. Light one lamp with one cell, then add a resistor to the same loop. What happens to the lamp?

Hint

Drop the resistor onto one of the wires and it joins into the loop.

The lamp gets dimmer. The resistor makes it harder for the current to flow, so the current is smaller.

8.Add an ammeter to the loop and read the current. Then take the resistor out, join the gap with a wire, and read it again. Type both currents.

Hint

To take the resistor out, tap it and press Delete. Then drag a wire across the gap it leaves.

With the 10 Ω resistor the current is 0.10 A; without it, 0.30 A. The bigger the resistance, the smaller the current, and it is smaller everywhere in the loop.

9.Press Clear. Light one lamp using two cells in series. How bright is the lamp compared with one cell?

Hint

Put the cells one after the other in the loop, with the long line of one cell joined to the short line of the next.

The lamp is brighter. Cells in series add their voltages: 1.5 V + 1.5 V = 3 V, so the current doubles to 0.60 A. That is how you would get enough voltage from fruit cells.

Challenge10.Wire it like the lights in a house. Use one cell, two lamps and two switches. Build a circuit where each switch turns off one lamp and leaves the other lamp on.

Put the lamps in parallel with a switch in each branch. Opening a switch only breaks its own branch, so the other lamp stays on.

Extra11.Press Clear. First predict: three lamps in parallel on one cell. What will an ammeter beside the cell read? Then build it and type the reading.

Hint

Each lamp on a branch of its own takes the same current as one lamp on its own.

The cell gives 0.90 A: 0.30 A for each of the three branches. Every lamp you add in parallel takes more current from the cell, which is why a cell runs flat faster.

Extra12.Press Clear. A string of Christmas lights: build three lamps in series on one cell, with an ammeter in the loop and a voltmeter across one lamp. Predict first, then type both readings.

Hint

Three lamps share the cell’s 1.5 V equally.

The current is 0.10 A and each lamp has 0.50 V: three equal shares of 1.5 V. More lamps in series means more resistance, so a smaller current and dimmer lamps.

Short circuit! The current has a path with almost no resistance, so it is far too big. A real cell would get hot and go flat quickly.

  • Add a component: drag it onto the grid, or tap it.
  • Put a component into a wire: drop it onto a straight part of the wire and it joins in. Or tap the wire, then tap the component.
  • Add a wire: drag from the end of a component to where the wire should go. Or tap the end, then tap where it should go.
  • Move, turn or delete: drag a component to move it. Tap it for Turn and Delete, or drag it off the grid.
  • Ammeter: measures current. It goes in series: drop it onto a wire so it is in the loop.
  • Voltmeter: measures voltage. It goes across a component (in parallel): one lead to each side of it.
  • Switches: tap a switch to open or close it.
  • The cells on this page are perfect cells, with no resistance inside them, so the readings add up exactly.
  • The moving dots show the electrons, which flow from the negative end of the cell (the short line) round to the positive end. Faster dots mean a bigger current. Show conventional flow turns the dots round, positive to negative: the direction used for current in later courses.
