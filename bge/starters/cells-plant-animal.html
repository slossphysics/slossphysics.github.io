BGE · Third level · Biological systems · SCN 3-13a

Plant and animal cells

LI: I am learning the parts of plant and animal cells and how to look at cells with a microscope.

SC: I can name the parts of a cell and say what each does · I can compare plant and animal cells · I can calculate the magnification of a microscope.

5:00

1.This is an animal cell. Match each numbered part to its name.

Drag each number onto its name, or tap a number and then its name.

The nucleus is the large round part. The cell membrane is the thin outer edge. The cytoplasm is the jelly that fills the cell. The small sausage shapes are mitochondria.

2.This is a plant cell. Match each numbered part to its name.

Drag each number onto its name, or tap a number and then its name.

Hint

The cell wall is the thick outer layer. The big space in the middle is filled with cell sap.

The thick outer layer is the cell wall. The small oval discs are chloroplasts. The large space in the middle is the vacuole. The round part is the nucleus.

3.Match each part of a cell to its job.

Drag each job onto a part, or tap a job and then a part.

Hint

The cell wall and the cell membrane are easy to mix up. Only one of them is stiff, like a box. The other is thin and lets some things through.

The nucleus is the control centre. The membrane lets some things in and out. The wall gives support. Chloroplasts make food and mitochondria release energy.

4.Sort these cell parts: are they found in plant cells only, or in both plant and animal cells?

Drag each part into its group, or tap it and then the group.

Both kinds of cell have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm and mitochondria. Only plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole. Plants need to release energy too, so they have mitochondria.

5.A cell from a plant's root has a cell wall and a large vacuole, but no chloroplasts. Why?

Chloroplasts need light for photosynthesis. Root cells are in the dark, so they don't have any. Not every plant cell has chloroplasts.

6.You put a slide of onion cells on a microscope. Which objective lens should you use first?

Start on low power: you see more of the slide, so the cells are easier to find and focus. Then move up to a higher power to see more detail.

7.Why do we add a drop of stain, such as iodine, to onion cells before looking at them?

Onion cells are almost see-through. A stain colours parts such as the nucleus so they show up clearly.

8.A microscope has a ×10 eyepiece lens and a ×40 objective lens. Calculate the total magnification.

You've added the two lenses. To get the total magnification, multiply them.

You've divided. To get the total magnification, multiply the two lenses.

Hint

Total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification.

  • total magnification = eyepiece × objective
  • total magnification = 10 × 40
  • total magnification = ×400

9.A microscope has a ×10 eyepiece lens. A pupil wants a total magnification of ×100. What magnification of objective lens should they use?

You've multiplied 10 by 100. Which number times 10 gives 100?

You've taken 10 away from 100, but the lenses multiply. Which number times 10 gives 100?

  • total magnification = eyepiece × objective
  • 100 = 10 × objective
  • objective = ×10

Challenge10.Tap all the statements that are true. There are 3.

Plants need energy too, so their cells have mitochondria. Only plant cells have a wall, and it sits outside the membrane, which both kinds of cell have. Cells in the dark, such as root cells, have no chloroplasts.
