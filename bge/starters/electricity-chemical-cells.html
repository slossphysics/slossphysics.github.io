Chemical cells

LI: I am learning how a simple chemical cell works and what affects its voltage.

SC: I can say what a chemical cell needs · I can name factors that change its voltage · I can plan a fair test.

5:00

1.What does a cell (battery) store?

A cell is a portable store of chemical energy. When it is in a circuit, that energy is transferred as electrical energy to the components.

2.To make a simple chemical cell, what do you need? Tap both.

A chemical cell needs two different metals (the electrodes) with an electrolyte between them.

3.Sort each material: would it be an electrode or an electrolyte in a chemical cell?

Drag each material into its group, or tap a material and then the group.

The electrodes are the two different metals. The electrolyte is the liquid (or juicy fruit) between them.

4.A pupil sets up this cell. The voltmeter reads 0 V. Why?

Hint

Think back to the two things every chemical cell needs. Does this cell have both of them?

A cell needs two different metals. Two copper strips give no voltage, however good the electrolyte is.

5.A lemon cell makes an LED light up. Which energy change happens inside the cell?

In the cell, chemical energy is transferred to electrical energy. The LED then transfers it to light.

6.Which of these could change the voltage of a lemon cell? Tap all 3.

Hint

Only the parts of the cell itself matter: the metals and the electrolyte. Which choices are about those?

The voltage depends on the metals, the electrolyte and its concentration, the distance between the metals and their surface area.

7.Sam wants to find out if the distance between the metals changes the voltage of a cell. To make it a fair test, what should Sam change?

In a fair test you change one thing only (here, the distance) and keep everything else the same: the metals, the electrolyte, its concentration and the surface area.

8.A class made zinc and copper cells in salt water. They changed how much salt was in 100 ml of water. What do the results show?

Salt (spoons)Voltage (V)
10.40
20.52
30.61
40.68
More salt means a more concentrated electrolyte, and the voltage increases each time.

Challenge9.One lemon cell gives 0.9 V. An LED needs at least 2 V to light. The cells are joined in series, so their voltages add up. What is the smallest number of lemon cells that will light the LED?

Two cells give 0.9 V + 0.9 V = 1.8 V. Is that enough for the LED?

You can't use part of a lemon cell. Round to a whole number of cells that gives at least 2 V.

Four cells would work, but is there a smaller number that still gives at least 2 V?

Hint

Add 0.9 V one cell at a time. Stop as soon as the total reaches 2 V or more.

3 cells. Two cells give 1.8 V, which is not enough. Three cells give 0.9 V + 0.9 V + 0.9 V = 2.7 V, which is enough to light the LED.
