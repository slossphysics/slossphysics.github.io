3.The battery supplies 12 V. Voltmeter P reads 5.0 V across the lamp and voltmeter Q reads 4.0 V across the motor. What does voltmeter R read across the resistor?

V

That’s the battery voltage. In series it is shared between the three components.

That’s P and Q added together. How much of the 12 V is left for the resistor?

The voltages across the components add up to 12 V, so R must be less than 12 V.

The three components are different, so they don’t share the 12 V equally. Use P and Q.

R isn’t the difference between P and Q. All three voltages add up to the battery voltage.

Hint

In a series circuit, what must P, Q and R add up to?

In series the voltages add up to the supply: 5.0 V + 4.0 V + R = 12 V, so R = 12 − 9.0 = 3.0 V.

4.The two lamps are different. Ammeter Z, next to the cell, reads 0.90 A. Ammeter X reads 0.40 A. What does ammeter Y read?

A

The current is only the same everywhere in a series circuit. Here the current from the cell splits between two branches.

The branch currents add up to Z, so Y must be less than 0.90 A.

The lamps are different, so the current doesn’t split equally. Use X’s reading.

The lamps are different, so the two branches don’t carry the same current.

Hint

The current from the cell splits between the two branches. What do X and Y add up to?

In parallel the branch currents add up to the current from the cell: 0.40 A + Y = 0.90 A, so Y = 0.50 A.

Looking ahead: have a guess

5.Four groups build the same series circuit with a battery, a lamp and an ammeter, using kit that looks identical. Their ammeters read 0.29 A, 0.31 A, 0.27 A and 0.30 A. Have a guess: tap the 2 most likely reasons the readings are not all the same.

Wires, connections, lamps and even ammeters all resist the current a little. This is called resistance, and it is never exactly the same in two circuits. Batteries vary too. So real results come out slightly different, even when they “should” be the same.

6.A wire carrying a big current gets warm. Have a guess: where does the heat come from?

Hint

Current is never used up. What else does the supply give the charges, which can change from one form into another?

As the charges flow, they bump into the particles of the wire. The wire resists the current, and some electrical energy changes into heat. The current itself is not used up.

7.Resistance changes some electrical energy into heat. Sometimes that heat is the whole point. Sort each one: is the heat the useful job, or wasted?

Drag each card into its group, or tap a card and then the group.

Kettles, radiators and toasters have a heating element: a wire designed to resist the current and turn electrical energy into heat on purpose. In chargers, laptops and cables the heat is wasted energy.

The two lamps in Y’s branch are in series, so they each carry Y’s current. The question asks about the top lamp.

Hint

The current from the battery splits between the two branches. What must the top branch and Y add up to?

The branch currents add up to Z: I + 0.20 A = 0.60 A, so I = 0.40 A. The top branch has only one lamp, so it is easier for the current to flow there: it carries twice as much as the branch with two lamps.
