Resistance makes it harder for current to flow. More resistance means a smaller current and a dimmer lamp. Less resistance means a bigger current and a brighter lamp.

2.A variable resistor lets you turn something up and down smoothly. A switch can only turn it on or off. Sort these.

Drag each one into its group, or tap it and then the group.

Anything you can turn up and down a little at a time needs a way to change the current smoothly: volume knobs, dimmers, joysticks and speed triggers. A doorbell and a kettle only need on or off, so a switch will do.

3.You turn a dimmer and the lamp gets brighter. What has happened in the circuit?

A dimmer is a variable resistor. Turning it up makes the resistance smaller, so a bigger current flows and the lamp is brighter.

4.A variable resistor and a lamp are in series. The first ammeter reads 0.2 A. What does the second ammeter read?

Hint

Follow the wire round with your finger. Is there anywhere the current could split off?

In a series circuit there is only one path, so the current is the same everywhere: 0.2 A.

5.Two lamps are connected in parallel with a 9 V battery. What is the voltage across each lamp?

Hint

Each lamp is on its own branch, joined straight to the two ends of the battery.

In a parallel circuit every branch gets the full supply voltage: 9 V across each lamp.

Challenge6.Lamp X has a variable resistor on its branch. Lamp Y is on a branch of its own. The resistance is turned up. What happens to the lamps?

Hint

Take one branch at a time. Each branch is joined straight to the battery, so ask: has anything on this branch changed?

More resistance on X's branch means a smaller current through X, so X gets dimmer. Y's branch still has the full battery voltage and nothing on it has changed, so Y stays the same. This is why dimming one light at home doesn't dim the rest.
