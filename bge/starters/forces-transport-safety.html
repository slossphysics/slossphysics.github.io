BGE · Fourth level · Forces, electricity and waves · SCN 4-07b

Transport safety

LI: I am learning how physics is used to make road transport safer.

SC: I can work out a stopping distance · I can say what affects thinking distance and braking distance · I can explain how seatbelts, airbags and crumple zones reduce the force on passengers.

5:00

1.A driver sees a hazard and stops the car. What is the car's stopping distance?

Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. The thinking distance is how far the car goes while the driver reacts. The braking distance is how far it goes once the brakes are on.

2.Sort each card: does it make the thinking distance longer, the braking distance longer, or both?

Drag each card into its group, or tap a card and then the group.

Hint

Thinking distance depends on the driver's reactions. Braking distance depends on the car and the road.

Thinking distance is longer when the driver reacts slowly: tired, distracted by a phone, or after alcohol. Braking distance is longer when there is less grip or worse brakes: wet or icy roads, worn tyres, worn brakes. A higher speed makes both longer.

3.A driver sees a child step into the road. The thinking distance is 9 m and the braking distance is 14 m. Calculate the stopping distance.

m

You've subtracted. The car travels the thinking distance and then the braking distance, so add them.

You've multiplied. The car travels one distance and then the other, so add them.

That's only the braking distance. The car also moves while the driver is reacting.

  • stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
  • stopping distance = 9 + 14
  • stopping distance = 23 m

4.Which of these makes the braking distance longer, but does not change the thinking distance?

Ice means less friction between the tyres and the road, so the car takes further to stop once the brakes are on. The driver's reactions are not affected, so only the braking distance gets longer.

5.The diagram shows the stopping distance of a car. Calculate the braking distance.

m

You've added. The 53 m already includes the thinking distance, so take the thinking distance away.

You've divided. The stopping distance is made of two parts added together. What is left when you take one part away?

Hint

The whole bar is the stopping distance. The braking distance is what is left after the thinking distance.

  • braking distance = stopping distance − thinking distance
  • braking distance = 53 − 15
  • braking distance = 38 m

6.In a crash, how does a seatbelt help to protect a passenger?

Hint

Think about F = ma. If a passenger slows down over a longer time, what happens to their deceleration?

The seatbelt stretches slightly, so the passenger takes a longer time to stop. That means a smaller deceleration, and by F = ma a smaller force on their body. It also stops them hitting the windscreen or being thrown out.

7.Which of these car features protect people in a crash by making them take longer to stop? Tap all that apply.

Seatbelts stretch, airbags squash, and crumple zones fold up as the front of the car hits something. All three make the stopping time longer, so the force on the people inside is smaller.

8.Put these sentences in order to explain how an airbag protects a passenger. One sentence is wrong and is not needed.

Drag each sentence to its place, or tap a sentence and then its place.

Hint

Start with the crash itself. The last sentence should say what happens to the force on the passenger.

The car stops suddenly, the airbag inflates, and the passenger moves into it and squashes it. They take longer to stop, so their deceleration is smaller, and by F = ma the force on them is smaller. An airbag does not stop the passenger instantly: that would mean a huge force.

Challenge9.Two cars of the same mass crash into a wall at the same speed. Car P has a crumple zone, so its crash lasts 4 times as long as the crash in car Q, which has no crumple zone. Each car has a passenger of the same mass, wearing a seatbelt. Compared with the passenger in car Q, the force on the passenger in car P is about:

Hint

Deceleration = change in speed ÷ time. Then use F = ma for the same mass.

Both passengers lose the same speed. In car P it takes 4 times as long, so the deceleration (change in speed ÷ time) is 4 times smaller. The mass is the same, so by F = ma the force is also 4 times smaller.
