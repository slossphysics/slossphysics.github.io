BGE · Third level · Planet Earth · SCN 3-06a

Is there life out there?

LI: I am learning to use evidence to argue about whether there could be life elsewhere in the Universe.

SC: I can list the basic needs of living things · I can explain why a planet's distance from its star matters · I can pick out a well-supported argument.

5:00

1.Which of these do living things need to survive? Tap all that apply.

Living things need liquid water, nutrients (such as carbon and nitrogen), a source of energy (such as light from a star or heat from inside a planet) and a suitable temperature.

2.Why is a planet very close to its star unlikely to have liquid water?

Very close to a star it is too hot: water boils away into gas. Very far away it is too cold and water freezes into ice. Life needs water as a liquid.

3.Three rocky planets, A, B and C, orbit a star like the Sun. Which planet is most likely to have liquid water?

Hint

Think about how hot or cold each planet would be. Water needs to be not too hot and not too cold to stay liquid.

Planet B is in the habitable zone (the "Goldilocks zone"): not too hot and not too cold, so water can be liquid. A is too hot and C is too cold. Earth is in the Sun's habitable zone.

4.Astronomers are studying a new planet. Sort each finding: does it make life on the planet more likely or less likely?

Drag each card into its group, or tap a card and then the group.

More likely: liquid water, nutrients (carbon and nitrogen) and a steady source of energy. Less likely: far too hot, far too cold, or no water at all.

5.None of these places is likely to have life like Earth's. Match each one to the main reason why.

Drag each place onto its reason, or tap a place and then its reason.

Venus is the hottest planet because its thick atmosphere traps heat. Mercury is closest to the Sun but has almost no atmosphere, so it swings between very hot and very cold. Neptune is a freezing giant planet. Our Moon has no air and no liquid water.

6.There are more than 100 billion stars in our galaxy alone, and billions of galaxies in the Universe. Why does this make life elsewhere more likely?

Astronomers have found thousands of planets around other stars, and many stars have planets. With so many stars, even if only a tiny fraction have a planet in the habitable zone, that is still a huge number of planets where life might be possible.

7.Four pupils give their views on life elsewhere. Which is the best-supported argument?

The best argument uses evidence (the huge number of stars and planets) and is honest about what we don't know yet. The others jump to a conclusion that the evidence doesn't support.

8.Scientists have found ice at the poles of Mars and under its surface. Why are they excited about this?

Ice is frozen water. If it melts, even underground, there could be liquid water, one of the needs of life. That makes Mars a good place to search for signs of life, past or present.

Challenge9.Planet X is in its star's habitable zone, but it is a giant ball of gas with no solid surface and no liquid water. Moon Y orbits a giant planet outside the habitable zone. It has an ocean of liquid water under its icy crust, kept warm by heat from inside the moon. Which statement is best supported?

Hint

Go through the needs of life one at a time: liquid water, energy, nutrients, a suitable temperature. Which place ticks more of them?

The habitable zone matters because it is where water can be liquid, but it is not the whole story. Moon Y has liquid water and an energy source (heat from inside), so it may be more likely to support life. Real moons such as Jupiter's Europa and Saturn's Enceladus are like this, so scientists plan to search them for life.
