BGE · Fourth level · Planet Earth · SCN 4-06a

Seeing the invisible Universe

LI: I am learning how new ways of observing space have changed what we know about the Universe.

SC: I can explain why some telescopes must be in space · I can use line spectra to find the elements in a star · I can put key discoveries in order and explain why they mattered.

5:00

1.Radio telescopes can work on the ground, even on a cloudy day. Why?

Radio waves pass straight through the atmosphere and clouds, so radio telescopes work on the ground, day or night, in any weather. They use huge dishes to collect the very weak signals.

2.Sort each type of radiation: can a telescope detect it from the ground, or must the telescope be in space?

Drag each card into its group, or tap a card and then the group.

Hint

Think about what we can see or pick up here on the ground, for example with our eyes or a radio.

Radio waves and visible light pass through the atmosphere, so they can be detected from the ground. X-rays and gamma rays are absorbed by the atmosphere (which protects us), so those telescopes must be in space.

3.Astronomers spread a star's light out into a spectrum and find a pattern of lines. What do the lines tell them?

Each element gives its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint or barcode. Matching the lines tells astronomers which elements are in the star, without ever going there.

4.This is the line spectrum of a star.

These are the line spectra of four elements, W, X, Y and Z. Which elements are in the star? Tap all that apply.

Hint

An element is in the star only if every one of its lines appears in the star's spectrum, in exactly the same place.

All the lines of X and of Z appear in the star's spectrum, and together they make up all six of its lines. W has one line that is missing, and Y's lines don't match at all.

5.In 2015, scientists detected gravitational waves for the first time. What made them?

Gravitational waves are ripples in space itself. The first ones were detected in 2015 from two black holes colliding very far away. Einstein had predicted them about 100 years earlier. Black holes give out no light, so this was a whole new way of observing the Universe.

6.Put these discoveries in order, from earliest to most recent.

Drag each event to its place, or tap an event and then its place.

Hint

Think about the technology each one needed, and roughly when it was invented.

Galileo's moons of Jupiter (1610), Newton's reflecting telescope (1668), radio waves from the Milky Way (1930s), Hubble launched (1990), gravitational waves (2015), James Webb launched (2021).

7.In 1610, many people believed that everything in the sky orbited the Earth. Galileo saw four moons orbiting Jupiter. Why was this such important evidence?

The moons were orbiting Jupiter, so not everything orbits the Earth. This new evidence supported the idea that the Earth and planets orbit the Sun. New observations can change what scientists accept.

8.The James Webb Space Telescope mainly detects infrared radiation. Why is it in space rather than on the ground? Tap all that apply.

Water vapour in the atmosphere absorbs much of the infrared from space. Also, warm things give out infrared, so the warm Earth and air would drown out faint signals. In space, far from Earth and shaded from the Sun, Webb can stay very cold and detect them.

Challenge9.People have studied the sky with light for thousands of years. Why have radio telescopes, X-ray telescopes and gravitational wave detectors led to so many new discoveries?

Hint

Think about the gravitational waves detected in 2015. Could an ordinary telescope have seen what made them?

Many objects and events give out little or no visible light: colliding black holes, very hot gas that gives out X-rays, cold clouds that give out radio waves. Each new kind of detector lets us observe things that were invisible before, adding to our knowledge of the Universe.
