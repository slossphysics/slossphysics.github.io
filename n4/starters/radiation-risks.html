N4 · Waves and Radiation · Nuclear radiation

Weighing up the risk

LI: I am learning to compare the risk from nuclear radiation with other hazards, and how the risks are managed.

SC: I can use time, distance and shielding to reduce risk · I can compare radiation with other hazards · I can explain why we accept some risks.

5:00

1.There are three ways to reduce the risk from a radioactive source: less time near it, more distance from it, and shielding between you and it. Sort each safety rule by the way it works.

Drag each rule into its group, or tap it and then the group.

Time: the less time near a source, the smaller the dose. Distance: the further away, the less radiation reaches you. Shielding: lead and concrete absorb radiation before it reaches you.

2.Why must pupils never eat or drink in a lab where radioactive sources are used?

A radioactive substance inside the body keeps giving out radiation right next to your cells, and you can't move away from it. Keeping food and drink out of the lab stops this happening.

3.Some homes in Scotland have high levels of radon gas. What is the best way to reduce the risk?

Hint

Radon is a gas that seeps up from the ground. How could you stop it from building up inside a house?

Radon seeps up from the ground and builds up indoors. A small fan under the floor carries it outside, and fresh air stops it collecting in rooms.

4.Make a guess: which of these is the biggest risk to a person's health?

Smoking is by far the biggest risk here. It causes many thousands of deaths in the UK every year. The others add only tiny extra doses of radiation. People often worry most about radiation, even when other hazards are far more dangerous.

5.Which risks come from burning fossil fuels such as coal and gas to generate electricity? Tap all that apply.

Burning fossil fuels gives out carbon dioxide, which causes global warming, and smoke that causes air pollution. Surprisingly, coal also makes radioactive waste: it contains tiny amounts of uranium and thorium, which last for billions of years and end up in the ash. A coal power station releases more radioactivity into its surroundings than a nuclear one making the same energy, although nuclear waste is far more concentrated and dangerous. Every way of generating electricity has some risk: the question is which risks we choose, and how well we manage them.

6.True or false? Because nuclear radiation can be dangerous, the safest choice is never to use it at all.

Radiation saves lives, for example by treating cancer and sterilising medical equipment. When the risks are managed carefully, the benefits can be much bigger than the risk. We are exposed to background radiation anyway.

7.During radiotherapy for cancer, the hospital staff leave the room while the patient is treated. Why?

Hint

Who benefits from the radiation? And who would be near it again and again, all day, every day?

For the patient, the benefit is worth the risk. The staff get no benefit, and their doses would add up over many treatments every day, so they reduce their risk using distance and shielding.

Challenge8.A headline says: “Radiation found in Brazil nuts!” Brazil nuts do contain tiny amounts of radioactive substances. What is the most sensible response?

Hint

Everyone receives background radiation all the time, partly from food. Is a handful of nuts likely to add much to that?

The dose from a handful of Brazil nuts is tiny compared with the background radiation everyone receives every year. Something can contain radioactive substances and still carry a very small risk. Comparing risks helps us decide what is worth worrying about.
