N4 · Waves and Radiation · Nuclear radiation

Uses of nuclear radiation

LI: I am learning how nuclear radiation is used in medicine and industry.

SC: I can sort uses into medical and industrial · I can explain how a tracer and a thickness gauge work · I can tell nuclear radiation apart from other kinds of radiation.

5:00

1.Sort each use of nuclear radiation: medical or industrial?

Drag each use into its group, or tap it and then the group.

Medical uses help to diagnose and treat patients, or keep equipment free of germs. Industrial uses help to make, check and power things.

2.Which of these use nuclear radiation? Tap all that apply.

Hint

Nuclear radiation comes from the nucleus of a radioactive substance. Microwaves and radio waves are a different kind of radiation: part of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Cancer treatment, smoke alarms and checking welds all use a radioactive source. Microwave ovens and phones use electromagnetic waves, which don't come from the nucleus.

3.A patient is given a tiny amount of a radioactive tracer. How do doctors use it?

The tracer gives out radiation that passes out of the body. A detector outside tracks where it goes, so doctors can see whether an organ is working properly or a blood vessel is blocked.

4.A paper mill uses this thickness gauge. Radiation from the source passes through the paper to the detector. The detector reading suddenly goes down. What has happened to the paper?

Hint

The paper absorbs some of the radiation. Would thicker paper let more or less radiation through to the detector?

Thicker paper absorbs more radiation, so less reaches the detector and the reading goes down.

5.A water company thinks a pipe buried under a road is leaking. How can nuclear radiation help them find the leak without digging up the whole road?

This is a tracer again. Water carrying the radioactive substance escapes at the leak and soaks into the soil. A detector above ground picks up more radiation at that spot.

6.Plastic syringes are sterilised with nuclear radiation instead of being heated. Why?

Radiation kills germs without heating, so plastic doesn't melt. It passes through the sealed packet, so the syringes stay germ-free until they are opened.

7.True or false? Some smoke alarms in homes contain a small radioactive source.

Some smoke alarms contain a tiny, sealed radioactive source. Its radiation lets a tiny current flow through the air inside. Smoke gets in the way, the current drops, and the alarm sounds.

Challenge8.In this paper mill, a computer moves the rollers to keep the paper the right thickness. The detector reading goes up. What should the rollers do?

Hint

Two steps. First: if more radiation is reaching the detector, is the paper too thick or too thin? Second: how should the rollers move to fix that?

A higher reading means more radiation is getting through, so the paper is too thin. The rollers move further apart to make it thicker again.
