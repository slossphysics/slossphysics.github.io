Room 2 of 5 · Radiation

Reactor bay

A cracked radioactive source is leaking in the reactor bay. Work out what it's giving out and keep the crew safe.

1.What is an alpha particle?

An alpha particle is two protons and two neutrons. It's big, slow and charged, which is why it ionises so strongly but can't get far.

2.Radiation from the leak can ionise atoms in the crew's bodies. What does ionisation mean?

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. Knock an electron off (or add one) and it becomes a charged ion. This is how radiation damages living cells.

3.The bay's sensors fire three kinds of radiation at three absorbers. Match each kind of radiation to its ray.

Drag each name onto its ray, or tap a name and then a ray.

Hint

Which kind of radiation is the most penetrating? That one gets the furthest.

Alpha is stopped by paper (or a few centimetres of air). Beta is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Gamma is only reduced by thick lead or concrete. The most penetrating, gamma, is the least ionising; alpha is the most ionising.

4.A spare gamma source has to be stored safely. Which container should it be kept in?

Gamma is the most penetrating radiation, so it needs thick lead (or concrete) to cut it down.

5.A detector next to the cracked source gives these readings. The background count is 20 counts per minute. What does the source give out?

The background count is there all the time, even with no source. Which absorber made the count drop to background?

Paper made no difference, so there's no alpha. 5 mm of aluminium brought the count down to background, so everything from the source was stopped: there's no gamma either. The source gives out beta only.

3.A thruster pushes exhaust gas backwards out of the station. What makes the station move forwards?

Newton's third law: if A pushes on B, then B pushes back on A with an equal force in the opposite direction. The thruster pushes the gas backwards, so the gas pushes the thruster, and the station, forwards.

N

3.A satellite is moved from a low orbit to a higher orbit. What happens to its period (the time it takes to go round once)?

Higher orbits have longer periods. Low satellites like the ISS go round in about 90 minutes; geostationary satellites, 36 000 km up, take 24 hours.

4.The star chart gives distances in light-years. What is a light-year?

A light-year is a distance: about 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m. Astronomers use it because the distances between stars are so huge.

5.As the escape pod comes back through Earth's atmosphere, its outside gets extremely hot. Why?

The pod rubs against the air as it falls, which slows it down. Its kinetic energy is changed into heat, so it needs a heat shield to protect the crew.

Power

Power is how much energy is transferred every second.

P = Et

  • P is power, in watts (W)
  • E is energy, in joules (J)
  • t is time, in seconds (s)

A power of 1 W means 1 J of energy is transferred every second. So a 60 W lamp transfers 60 J of energy every second.

Worked example. A 50 W heater is switched on for 20 s. Calculate the energy it transfers.

  • P = E / t
  • 50 = E / 20
  • E = 50 × 20 = 1000 J

Watch out: time must be in seconds (1 minute = 60 s) and power in watts (1 kW = 1000 W).

1.The pod's cabin light has a power rating of 40 W. What does this mean?

40 W means 40 joules every second. In one minute (60 s) it transfers 40 × 60 = 2400 J.

2.Match each quantity to its unit.

Drag each quantity onto its unit, or tap a quantity and then its unit.

Power in watts, energy in joules, time in seconds. A watt is one joule per second.

3.A charger transfers 3000 J of energy to the pod's battery in 60 s. Calculate the power of the charger.

W

You've multiplied. P = E / t means divide the energy by the time.

You've divided the time by the energy. It's the other way round: P = E / t.

  • P = E / t
  • P = 3000 / 60
  • P = 50 W
