N5 · Radiation · Nuclear radiation

Coal, fission or fusion?

LI: I am recalling fission, fusion and half-life, and comparing the downsides of different energy sources.

SC: I can say what nuclear fission is · I can give a difficulty with fusion · I can calculate an activity using half-life · I can match downsides to coal, fission and fusion · I can write my answers in my jotter.

8:00

1.What is meant by the term nuclear fission? Write your answer in your jotter first, then tap the best match.

In nuclear fission, a large nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and 2 or 3 neutrons. Always say nucleus: “an atom splits” gets no mark in the exam.

2.Tap the statements that are difficulties in keeping nuclear fusion going in a reactor. There are 2.

Fusion needs very high temperatures (about 100 million °C), so the fuel is a plasma that is hard to contain and has to be held away from the walls by strong magnetic fields. Any one of these gets the mark. “It costs too much” on its own gets no mark.

3.A radioactive source has an activity of 1200 Bq. Its half-life is 4 hours. Calculate its activity after 12 hours.

Bq

You've divided by 3. Each half-life halves the activity: halve it once for each half-life.

That's after one half-life. How many half-lives are there in 12 hours?

That's after two half-lives. How many half-lives are there in 12 hours?

That's one halving too many. Count the half-lives again: 12 ÷ 4.

The activity goes down, not up. Halve it once for each half-life.

Hint

First work out how many half-lives fit into 12 hours.

Another hint

Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 4. Now halve 1200 Bq that many times.

  • Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 4 = 3
  • 1200 Bq → 600 Bq → 300 Bq → 150 Bq
  • Activity = 150 Bq

4.Match each downside to its energy source. Each source is used twice.

Drag each downside into its group, or tap it and then the group.

Coal: carbon dioxide, and (surprise!) the most radioactivity into the air. Fission: long-lasting radioactive waste, and the risk of a meltdown. Fusion: 100 million °C, and no power station makes electricity from it yet.

5.A coal power station puts more radioactivity into the air around it than a nuclear power station making the same amount of electricity. Why?

Coal is dug out of the ground, so it has tiny amounts of radioactive uranium and thorium in it. Burning coal leaves them in the ash, and some fine ash goes up the chimney. The dose to people nearby is still tiny: far less than background radiation.

Challenge6.Which statement is correct?

Nuclear waste is far more radioactive, kilogram for kilogram, but it is sealed away and guarded. Coal ash is only weakly radioactive, but there is a lot of it, and some goes straight up the chimney. That is why a coal station puts more radioactivity into the air around it.
