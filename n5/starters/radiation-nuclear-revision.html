Nuclear revision

LI: I am revising the nuclear topic with exam-style questions.

SC: I can explain a chain reaction · I can say why fusion is hard to keep going · I can do half-life calculations with prefixes · I can add up equivalent doses · I can set out my working in my jotter.

7:00

1.In a nuclear reactor, a neutron splits a uranium-235 nucleus. How can this single reaction lead to the continuous generation of energy?

Each fission releases neutrons, which go on to split other nuclei (1 mark). This keeps repeating: a chain reaction (1 mark). This is 2021 Q13(a)(ii), worth 2 marks.

2.Tap the reasons why nuclear fusion is hard to keep going in a reactor. There are 2.

Fusion needs extremely high temperatures, so the fuel is a plasma, which is very hard to contain: strong magnetic fields hold it away from the walls. In the exam (2022 Q14(b)(ii)), write one difficulty when it asks for one: “it costs too much” on its own gets no mark.

3.The activity of a radioactive source falls from 200 kBq to 25 kBq in 12 days. Calculate the half-life of the source.

days

That's the number of half-lives. Now share the 12 days between them.

That's half of the time. Keep halving 200 kBq until you reach 25 kBq, and count the arrows.

200 ÷ 25 = 8 is not the number of half-lives. Keep halving 200 until you reach 25, and count the arrows.

You've multiplied. The 12 days are shared between the half-lives.

That's the activity at the end. The question asks for a time.

Hint

Work backwards: write 200 kBq and keep halving until you reach 25 kBq.

Another hint

200 kBq, then 100 kBq, then 50 kBq, then 25 kBq. Count the arrows: that is the number of half-lives.

Another hint

Those half-lives took 12 days altogether. Share the 12 days equally between them.

  • 200 kBq → 100 kBq → 50 kBq → 25 kBq
  • 3 half-lives = 12 days
  • Half-life = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 days

4.A beta source has an activity of 12 kBq. Its half-life is 36 hours. Calculate its activity 144 hours later. Give your answer in becquerels.

Bq

That's right in kilobecquerels. The question asks for becquerels: 1 kBq = 1 × 103 Bq.

That's three halvings. How many lots of 36 hours fit into 144 hours?

That's five halvings. How many lots of 36 hours fit into 144 hours?

That's two halvings, or 12 000 divided by 4. There are 4 half-lives: halve 4 times.

You've divided the activity by the time. Work out the number of half-lives first, then halve that many times.

The activity of a source goes down as time passes, not up.

Hint

Change 12 kBq into becquerels first: 12 × 103 Bq.

Another hint

Number of half-lives = 144 ÷ 36. Draw that many arrows after 12 000 Bq.

Another hint

Halve at each arrow: 12 000 Bq, then 6000 Bq, and keep going until you have used all four arrows.

Number of half-lives = 144 ÷ 36 = 4

  • 12 × 103 Bq → 6000 Bq → 3000 Bq → 1500 Bq → 750 Bq
  • Activity after 144 hours = 750 Bq

5.During one shift, a worker at a nuclear power station receives an absorbed dose of 2.2 µGy from slow neutrons (wR = 3) and 3.4 µGy from gamma rays (wR = 1). Calculate the worker's total equivalent dose. Type powers of ten like this: 2.5x10-4.

Sv

That's right in microsieverts. The answer box is in sieverts: 1 µSv = 1 × 10−6 Sv.

You've added the absorbed doses. Each one needs multiplying by its own weighting factor first.

You've multiplied both doses by 3. Gamma rays have a weighting factor of 1.

That's the equivalent dose from the neutrons only. Add on the gamma rays' equivalent dose.

That's the weighting factor for fast neutrons (10). These are slow neutrons: wR = 3.

Check the prefix: µ (micro) is × 10−6, not × 10−3.

Hint

Work out an equivalent dose for each type of radiation with H = DwR, then add them.

Another hint

Neutrons: H = 2.2 × 10−6 × 3. Gamma: H = 3.4 × 10−6 × 1.

Another hint

Add the two equivalent doses. Your answer should be in sieverts, so keep the × 10−6.

  • H = DwR
  • Neutrons: H = 2.2 × 10−6 × 3 = 6.6 × 10−6 Sv
  • Gamma: H = 3.4 × 10−6 × 1 = 3.4 × 10−6 Sv
  • Total H = 6.6 × 10−6 + 3.4 × 10−6 = 1.0 × 10−5 Sv

Challenge6.A worker at a nuclear power station receives an equivalent dose of 1.0 × 10−5 Sv in each shift. The annual limit for this worker is 20 mSv. Calculate how many shifts it would take to reach the annual limit.

Check the prefix on the limit: 20 mSv is 20 × 10−3 Sv, not 20 Sv.

Check the powers of ten. 20 mSv = 20 × 10−3 Sv, and each shift is 1.0 × 10−5 Sv.

You've divided the wrong way up. Divide the annual limit by the dose in one shift.

Check the powers of ten: 10−3 ÷ 10−5 = 102, so 20 × 102.

Hint

Change 20 mSv into sieverts: 20 × 10−3 Sv.

Another hint

Number of shifts = annual limit ÷ dose in one shift.

  • Number of shifts = annual limit ÷ dose per shift
  • = 20 × 10−3 ÷ 1.0 × 10−5
  • = 2000 shifts
